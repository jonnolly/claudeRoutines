# Tip 002 — Don't migrate your task list. Restructure it into a queue an agent can pull from.

**Theme:** delegation infrastructure · **Time:** ~15 min (a decision + a template, not the migration)
**Why you:** you already do the hard part of this by accident

## The moment

You're about to move work tasks from TickTick to Todoist. Most people port 1:1, and the migration
pays exactly once — same list, new logo. There's a decision worth making *before* you start that
makes it pay every week afterwards.

Two facts make this worth 15 minutes of your time right now:

1. **Doist ships an official MCP server** (`github.com/Doist/todoist-mcp`). Once you're on Todoist,
   Claude can read *and write* your real work tasks. Your task list stops being a private notepad
   and becomes a shared work surface.
2. **You already write tasks better than almost anyone.** I read your old task bodies — exact
   filename grammar, a worked example, acceptance criteria, an explicit stop condition
   ("if you can't, you have to let me know the new start-time"). That's a brief, not a reminder.
   You do deliberately-good specification without calling it that.

Right now that skill is wasted, because a well-specified task sitting in a list nothing can read
is just a well-specified task. The migration is your one cheap chance to fix both ends at once.

## The 15 minutes

**1. Decide your agent-ready label (2 min).** You already had an `@claude` tag in TickTick — the
instinct was right, the semantics were wrong. `@claude` marked tasks *about* Claude. What you want
is a label that means **"specified well enough that something could start on this without asking
me a question."** Call it `@ready`. It's a queue, not a topic.

The discipline that makes it work: a task only earns the label once its body answers *what done
looks like*. Unlabelled is fine and normal — it just means "not briefed yet".

**2. Write your task template (8 min).** Three lines in the body, every time:

```
Outcome:  what is true when this is finished
Done when: the check that proves it — a test, a file, a sent email, a number
Context:  the one link / file / thread needed to start
```

You already write this. Making it a template means you write it *without deciding to*, and it
means anything reading your list knows where to look.

**3. Don't carry the dead across (5 min).** Your old projects were last touched in October 2025 —
roughly eleven months of tasks that survived only because moving them was easier than deciding
about them. A migration is a forcing function. Anything you wouldn't re-add today doesn't get a
seat on the boat.

## Why this one, and why now

You asked about multiple agents, assigning roles, having them continuously working. This is the
prerequisite, and it's the part nobody mentions.

**A continuously-working agent needs something to pull from.** Not a chat prompt — a queue of work
that's specified well enough to start on, in a system it can read and write, with a signal for what
"done" means. That's precisely what steps 1–3 build. Skip it and "agents working continuously"
collapses into you hand-feeding tasks one at a time in a chat window, which is just normal work
with extra latency.

So this is the same lesson as Tip 001, one level up. Tip 001 was: give a *requirement* a mechanical
definition of done. This is: give a *task* one. Same move, and both are what separate work you can
delegate from work you have to supervise.

## The 30-second version

Migrating is a chance to change the shape of the list, not just its address. Add one label meaning
"briefed well enough to start without me", put `Done when:` in every task body, and leave the
eleven-month-old tasks behind. That turns your backlog from a list you read into a queue something
else can work.

## When you're done

Tell me the migration's finished and I'll connect to Todoist and start reading actual work tasks —
at which point these tips stop being inferred from your email traffic and start being aimed at what
you're really doing this week.
